Reading the Table: Can a Model Price a Blackjack Hand?

How to read the table. The forecast is what the trained models say at the moment of the deal. They use the same inputs as the study: your two cards, the dealer's up-card, and the true count before the deal. Like the study, it assumes you will follow basic strategy. If you deviate, the forecast no longer describes your hand.

The live simulation is not a model. It replays the exact situation thousands of times from the cards that remain, so it shows the probability the models are trying to estimate.

The forecast is a probability, not a prediction of one hand. The check under the table compares the average forecast with how often you actually win, and that comparison becomes meaningful only over many hands.

The strategy card

The chance of winning, in percent, for every starting hand against every dealer up-card. It comes from 800,000 training rounds played with basic strategy, and a push counts as not a win. Select any cell to open it in the hand explorer further down.

Question

Under fixed casino rules and a fixed basic-strategy player, how accurately can a calibrated model estimate the chance that the player wins a round? It may use only what is visible right after the deal: the player's two-card hand (total, soft or hard, pair or not), the dealer's up-card, and the player's imperfect estimate of how many more high cards than low cards are left. We also ask how much of that accuracy comes from the deck estimate rather than the cards on the table.

One row is one round, recorded after the player's two cards and the dealer's up-card are on the table and before the player decides anything. The target is win = 1 if the round's money result is above zero, otherwise 0. A push is 0, and split hands are added up first.

Why the count is the "high-to-low" estimate from our proposal. A shoe starts with as many low cards (2–6) as high cards (10, A). So the Hi-Lo running count is exactly high cards left minus low cards left, and dividing by the decks left gives the excess of high cards per remaining deck. The player's version is taken before the deal and is deliberately noisy (see Data).

What "how accurately" means. A model's expected log-loss splits into two parts. One is the randomness of the hole card and the cards still to come, which no model can remove. The other is the model's own error: how far its probabilities sit from the true ones. Because we simulate, we can estimate the true probabilities from a separate 20-million-round run and measure both parts. That turns the question into three smaller ones:

  1. How close does each model get to the true probabilities, compared with a plain lookup table of hand against up-card?
  2. How much does the deck estimate add beyond the cards on the table?
  3. How much is lost because the player's count is imperfect?

Limits of the claim. All numbers hold only for one rule set (6 decks, dealer stands on soft 17, blackjack pays 3:2, double after split, one split, no surrender), for basic strategy, and for our model of an imperfect counter. Without a fixed playing policy the chance of winning is not even defined, since it depends on how the hand is played. Count-based playing changes, bet sizing, real casinos and causal claims are all out of scope.

Data

We wrote our own simulator (src/simulate.py), so there are no licence or privacy limits and anyone can rebuild the same rows from the seed. Does the simulator play real blackjack? The player's average result is −0.32% of the bet (95% CI −0.54% to −0.09%). Published house edge for these rules is about 0.4%, inside that range. The edge also improves by roughly half a percent per count point, which is the usual rule of thumb.

The imperfect count. We use Hi-Lo (2–6 are +1, 7–9 are 0, tens and aces are −1). A real player gets it slightly wrong, so we add normal noise with standard deviation 2 to the running count and let the player guess the decks left only to the nearest half deck. The estimate still correlates 0.96 with the exact true count, with a typical error of 0.69 points.

Rare hands. The 340 combinations of hand type and up-card are far from balanced. The median one has 783 training rounds; pair of 7s against a 7 has only 287. For cells like that, a simple win rate has an error of around 3 percentage points.

Preprocessing

The simulator writes complete rows, so there is nothing to clean. The fixed transforms in src/features.py turn the two cards into one of 34 hand types (hard 5 to 19, soft 13 to 20, blackjack, ten pairs) and cut the count into 9 bins one point wide. Neither looks at outcomes, so applying them before the split is safe.

Everything that is learned, like table rates, shrinkage targets, logistic weights and tree splits, happens inside each model's fit() on training shoes only. During cross-validation that means the training folds only. We did not scale the count, because the trees do not care and the raw count keeps the logistic weights readable as "log-odds per count point". The proposal's idea of regularising for collinear deck features did not survive: one count plus the state is not collinear.

Baseline and methods

Improving on the constant is easy. Improving on the plain table is the real test, because with 800k rows most of the 340 cells already have thousands of examples. A model can only improve on it by using the count well or by estimating rare cells better.

The tables use shrinkage: a cell with w wins out of n rounds gets (w + m·p0) / (n + m), where p0 is the parent rate. A big m trusts the parent, a small one trusts the cell.

Evaluation

Split by shoe. Rounds from the same shoe share a deck, so they are not independent. A random 20% of shoes (4,613 shoes, 200,054 rounds) form the test set. Every other shoe is for training. We dropped the stratified k-fold from our proposal for this reason: stratifying on the label does not fix the shoe dependence.

Tuning. Each model's settings are chosen by 5-fold cross-validation grouped by shoe, on training shoes only, by mean log-loss. The winner is refit on all training shoes and scored on the test shoes once. Side analyses (count ablation, learning curve) use a held-out training fold.

Metrics. Log-loss is the main one. We also report Brier score, calibration error (ECE, 20 equal-size groups) and AUC. We dropped F1: it needs a yes/no cut-off, and nothing here uses one.

Error bars. We resample the test shoes 2,000 times. Model-vs-table differences use the same resampled shoes for both, which is necessary to detect differences in the fourth decimal place. An interval that excludes zero says the gap is real for this rule set, this policy and this noise model. It says nothing about any other setup. We use intervals instead of p-values because the size of a gap matters more here than whether it exists.

Distance to the truth. Since we simulate, a separate 20M-round run gives a close estimate of the true win rate of every (cell, count bin). We report each model's average distance from it (RMSE, in percentage points), after subtracting the reference's own sampling noise. The same run gives the noise floor: the loss even a perfect model would get, because the hole card and the next cards are random.

What changed our minds. Our first quick run on a plain row split already had every model within 0.0002 log-loss of the others. The question of which model wins was therefore unlikely to be informative. That is why we added the noise floor and the reference run: to ask how close anyone can get, and what is left. The second surprise was the count chart in the Data section. The count moves the money about four times as much as the win rate, which explains why it adds so little here.

Results

All four models improve substantially on the constant, and they finish within 0.0001 log-loss of one another. The plain table is already only 0.0005 above the noise floor; it captures 99.4% of the improvement that is achievable at all.

Test shoes, scored once. Brackets are 95% paired shoe-bootstrap intervals. RMSE to reference is the average distance from the 20M-round estimate of the true win rate.

Against the noisy win/lose outcomes, only Table+count is clearly better than the table, and only marginally. The logistic model's gain is borderline on log-loss but clear on Brier. Measured against the reference truth, the ranking is clearer: the logistic model is 1.17 points from the true rate on average, against 1.47 for the table, about 20% closer. The trees come second.

Why accuracy is not used. Accuracy is reported here only to show why it cannot rank these models. Always guessing "not a win" is right 56.7% of the time. Every one of our models, thresholded at 50%, is right 67.7 to 67.8% of the time. A model that knew the true probabilities exactly would also score about 67.7%. By this measure all four models are already at the ceiling, so accuracy cannot separate them. This is why log-loss and distance to the truth are the metrics of this study.

Calibration

Pick a model to see how far its predictions sit from what actually happened. A perfectly calibrated model has every point on the zero line, give or take its error bar.

Does the count help, and does the imperfect estimate hurt?

It helps slightly, and the imperfection costs almost nothing. This is measured on a held-out training fold, so the test set stays untouched.

Using the exact count instead of the player's noisy estimate gains only about 0.02 × 10⁻³ log-loss, far inside the error bars. One caveat: the logistic model without the count is slightly worse than the table, because its regularisation pulls rare cells toward 50%. So part of its count "gain" just makes up for that.

Errors

Two quite different kinds of error appear in this problem, and they must be separated before any model can be judged.

Outcomes no model could see coming

These are the test rounds that surprised the logistic model most, with one row per hand. They are not model mistakes. An 11% chance should come true about 1 time in 9, and with 200,000 test rounds plenty of them do. Both lists still make sense. The left one is stiff hands against an ace at high counts: more tens are left, so the dealer is more likely to have blackjack and the model rightly goes low. The right one is strong hands at very negative counts, where the dealer still managed a tie or a better total.

Cells where a model really is off

To find real errors we compare each model's average prediction in a cell with the 20M-round reference. Two patterns show up. The tables and the logistic model miss in rare pair and soft cells, where 300 to 700 training rounds give an error of 2 to 3 points, so misses of 5 to 6 points are about what sampling noise predicts across 340 cells. The trees are steadier there, but are biased in cells like soft 18 against 8 and hard 19 against 9, where neighbouring totals behave very differently and a 15-leaf tree blurs them together. This is the bias–variance trade-off in concrete form.

The 8 cells with the largest gap for any model, in percentage points (prediction minus reference).

Where the count's effect is too small

The explorer shows a third kind of error. For hard 16 against a 10, the reference falls from about 21% at a count of −4 to 16% at +4, because more tens left means more busts when you hit. The logistic model only drops to 17.5%, and the trees and Table+count flatten out even more. Every model under-reacts at extreme counts. Those counts are rare inside any one cell, so shrinkage, regularisation and the minimum leaf size all pull the estimate back toward the cell's average. The logistic model's slope is also shared: it is the up-card's slope plus the hand's slope, with no separate slope for "16 against a 10". A per-cell slope would fix this, at the cost of 340 more weights.

Limitations

Everything is conditional on our setup. A different rule set, a count-aware playing strategy, or a different noise model for the counter would change the numbers. A player who changes their play with the count would see a bigger count effect than our basic-strategy player.

Win is a lossy target. Folding pushes into "not win" hides the part of the game the count affects most, the size of the payout. Expected return would be the better target for an actual betting decision.

The reference is an estimate too. It treats the true chance as flat inside each count bin and has its own sampling error. We subtract that error, but rare cells still have wide bands.

Simulated data. There is no human behaviour, no dealer error, no card-handling quirks. The question is about the game's mathematics, not about any real casino.

One noise setting. We picked a single level of counting error and never varied it. The ablation shows that the gap between "exact" and "estimated" is tiny at this level. Much sloppier counting could change that, and we have not tested it.

Small tuning grids. The logistic C landed on the edge of its grid. A larger C might be slightly better, though the differences are already tiny.

Conclusion

For predicting whether a blackjack round is won, the state on the table carries nearly all the information. A lookup table of hand against up-card gets within 0.0005 log-loss of a perfect model. The count adds a real but very small improvement, and the logistic model uses it best, ending up about 20% closer to the true probabilities than the table. Knowing the count exactly instead of approximately barely matters for this target. The count's real effect is on the money: it moves the average result about four times as much as the win rate. The interesting difference between models is data efficiency, not final accuracy: with 6k to 64k training rounds the boosted trees are clearly ahead of everything else, and the gap only closes once every cell is well filled.

What we would try next. Three things:

  • Give the logistic model a separate count slope per cell, to fix the under-reaction at extreme counts.
  • Repeat the study with expected return as the target, where the count matters far more.
  • Vary the counting noise, to find where "imperfect" starts to cost something.

Reproduce

Python 3.10 or newer, with numpy, scipy, pandas and scikit-learn (versions in requirements.txt). The full run takes around 10 minutes on one CPU core.

pip install -r requirements.txt
cd src
python simulate.py --rounds 1000000 --seed 335 --out ../data/hands.npz   # ~10 s
python reference.py                                                      # 20M rounds, ~2.5 min
python run.py                                                            # ~6 min, writes results/ and docs/data.js

The test shoes are listed in results/test_shoes.txt. A 50,000-row sample of the data is in data/sample_hands.csv for a quick look without running anything. The page you are reading is docs/: plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript reading docs/data.js, served by GitHub Pages.
